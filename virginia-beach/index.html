How Virginia Beach Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Many early failures start with maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Drain and flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard so the heat exchanger stays clean.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually encodes the month and year it was built, and that date says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is past that range and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or moisture at the base, it's a strong candidate for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A tank that's just begun to rumble needs only a flush, yet the same sediment, ignored for a few years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and burn out the lower element on an electric model, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number in your contacts now so you can reach us fast the moment something sounds or looks wrong. A quick call saves time, money, and ruined floors.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Around Virginia Beach, water heater trouble tends to trace back to a handful of sources. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Many homes in the area still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and once a tank is past about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust starts working on the steel from the inside. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: pilot assemblies, gas valves, and flues on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code is another piece. Swapping out an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: a thermal expansion tank if the plumbing is a closed system, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, including heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally shows up when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most cost far less when caught early.

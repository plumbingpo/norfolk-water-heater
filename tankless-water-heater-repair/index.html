The majority of tankless breakdowns announce themselves the same way, with a blinking code on the display and a shower that turns cold. Behind that code is a system with very little margin for error. There is no storage tank to fall back on, so the heater has to detect flow, light a burner or power its elements, and raise the water temperature by fifty degrees or more in the few seconds it takes water to cross the heat exchanger. When scale narrows the passages, when the flow sensor stops reading, when the flame rod cannot confirm a flame, or when the vent or gas line cannot keep up, the unit protects itself by shutting down. That is the pattern we see week after week in Norfolk, VA.

Error codes are a starting point, not a verdict, and we treat them that way on every Norfolk call. The same ignition failure code can come from a worn igniter, a fouled flame rod, a gas line too small to feed the burner at full fire, or an air intake clogged with lint. That is why Norfolk Water Heater tests before replacing anything. Our technicians read the fault history stored in the control board, watch flow sensor output with a faucet open, measure flame signal and igniter resistance, and put a manometer on the gas inlet to see what pressure actually reaches the valve when the burner is running flat out. We also compare inlet and outlet temperatures against the flow rate, which reveals a scaled heat exchanger long before it fails outright.

The good news is that most tankless problems are very fixable. A descaling flush clears most mineral buildup, sensors and igniters are replaceable parts, and venting faults usually come down to a blocked intake or a loose joint. We carry common parts for the major brands and service both gas and electric units, so the typical repair is done in one trip. We are also honest about the harder cases: a unit fed by a gas line too small for its full BTU rating will keep throwing codes until the line is upsized, and a heat exchanger damaged by scale may not be worth repairing on an older heater. And if your complaint is the cold-water sandwich, that brief burst of cold between two hot draws, we will tell you whether it is normal for your model or something we can reduce. When to Call for Tankless Water Heater Repair

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Leaks under the unit or around its water connections
  • A clicking igniter with no flame, or a flame that lights and quickly drops out
  • No hot water at a low-flow faucet even though the shower works fine
  • Hot water that cannot keep up with more than one fixture
  • A cold-water sandwich that has gotten noticeably worse
  • A fault code on the front panel and no hot water
  • New whistling, roaring, or rumbling from the unit
  • Hot water that fluctuates even though nobody touched the faucet

Why Hire a Professional for Tankless Water Heater Repair?

From the outside a tankless heater looks like a plain box; inside, it is a tightly controlled system. A single error code can come from four or five different faults, and the sensors, igniters, and control boards involved are not cheap to buy on a hunch. Gas models add real hazards: the burner can fire at close to 200,000 BTU per hour, the vent carries combustion gases that contain carbon monoxide, and a loose gas connection or a vent joint that separates can put fuel or exhaust into the living space. Electric models bring a different hazard, often drawing well over 100 amps across several 240-volt circuits, so a loose lug or undersized wire can overheat quickly.

A trained technician with proper test equipment gets to the answer faster. Ours read the fault history, measure flame signal and igniter resistance, test flow sensor output, and check gas pressure under full load with a manometer, which is the only reliable way to catch a gas line that holds pressure at idle but sags when the burner ramps up. Descaling is done with a circulating pump and the proper solution, followed by a thorough rinse. Just as important, you get an honest opinion: if the heat exchanger is damaged on an older unit, you will hear it from us before you spend money on a repair.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here's where to begin:

  • Never cut power to an outdoor or garage unit in winter, because it needs electricity to protect itself from freezing
  • Get the supply line checked before adding gas appliances, which can starve a tankless unit
  • Consider a water softener or scale-reduction filter ahead of the heater if scale keeps returning
  • Write down any error code before resetting the unit, and call if it keeps coming back
  • Flush scale out of the heat exchanger annually, and sooner in hard water
  • Keep the vent termination and air intake clear of leaves, nests, snow, and lint
  • Clean the inlet water filter screen a few times a year so the flow sensor gets full flow
